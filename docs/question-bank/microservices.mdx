## Microservices Fundamentals (7 questions)

<details id="what-is-a-microservice">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What is a microservice, and what makes it different from just "a service"?</summary>

A microservice is a small, independently deployable service that owns a single business capability
end-to-end — its own code, its own data, and its own release lifecycle — and communicates with
other services over the network. "Small" means small in scope (ownable by one team, changeable
without coordinating with others), not small in lines of code.

</details>

<details id="monolith-vs-microservices-vs-soa">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What's the difference between a monolith, microservices, and SOA?</summary>

A monolith is a single deployable unit where components talk via in-process calls and usually
share one database. Microservices are many small, independently deployable services, each owning
its own data, communicating over the network. SOA is the broader, older style microservices grew
out of, classically relying on a centralized Enterprise Service Bus for routing/orchestration,
with typically larger, more coarse-grained services than microservices use.

</details>

<details id="service-vs-module">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What's the difference between a service and a module/component?</summary>

A module (or component) is a unit of code organization within a single deployable — separated at
compile time, still running in the same process. A service is a unit of runtime and deployment
separation — it runs in its own process, is deployed independently, and is reached over the
network rather than a function call.

</details>

<details id="modular-monolith">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--edge">Edge Case</span>What is a modular monolith, and why might a team choose it over microservices?</summary>

A monolith with clean module boundaries and no cross-module database access. It gets much of the
maintainability benefit of service boundaries without the distributed-systems tax (network
latency, partial failure, distributed data), making it a reasonable middle ground for teams that
adopted microservices too early would have been better served by first.

</details>

<details id="bounded-context">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What is a bounded context, and why does it matter for drawing service boundaries?</summary>

A bounded context is an explicit boundary within which a domain model and its terminology are
consistent — the same word can mean different things in different contexts. The rule of thumb is
one microservice should map to at most one bounded context; splitting a context across services
tends to produce chatty, tightly-coupled services.

</details>

<details id="when-microservices">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>When should a team choose microservices over a monolith, and when shouldn't they?</summary>

Worth it when different parts need to scale independently, multiple teams need to ship without
blocking each other, or parts have genuinely different tech/reliability needs. Usually the wrong
call when coordination was never the bottleneck, domain boundaries aren't well understood yet, or
the team lacks the operational maturity to run many independently deployed services.

</details>

<details id="start-with-a-monolith">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--edge">Edge Case</span>Why is "start with a monolith" often considered good advice even for systems expected to scale?</summary>

Because service boundaries drawn before the domain's real seams are understood tend to be wrong,
producing chatty or tightly-coupled services. A well-factored monolith is cheaper to run and
refactor, and services should be extracted only when a specific, measured pain point justifies the
added operational cost.

</details>

## Service Communication (6 questions)

<details id="sync-vs-async">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What's the core trade-off between synchronous and asynchronous service communication?</summary>

Synchronous calls (REST, gRPC) block the caller until a response arrives — simple to reason about,
but couples the caller's availability to the callee's and stacks latency across chained calls.
Asynchronous messaging decouples services in time, improving resilience and throughput, at the
cost of an immediate response and having to reason about eventual consistency.

</details>

<details id="rest-vs-grpc">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What's the difference between REST and gRPC, and when would you pick one over the other?</summary>

REST uses human-readable JSON over HTTP with a loose contract and is browser-friendly; gRPC uses
binary Protobuf over HTTP/2 with a strict, code-generated contract and first-class streaming.
Common pattern: gRPC between internal services for performance, REST/JSON at the edge for browsers
and external clients.

</details>

<details id="message-queue-vs-event-stream">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What's the difference between a message queue and an event stream like Kafka?</summary>

A message queue (RabbitMQ, SQS) typically has a message consumed and removed by one consumer — good
for task distribution. An event stream (Kafka) appends events to a durable, ordered, replayable log
that multiple independent consumer groups can each read at their own pace — good for broadcasting
facts to many interested services.

</details>

<details id="orchestration-vs-choreography">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What's the difference between orchestration and choreography for multi-service workflows?</summary>

Orchestration has a central coordinator explicitly calling each service and directing the flow —
easy to follow, but the coordinator is a central dependency. Choreography has no coordinator; each
service reacts to others' events and publishes its own — no single point of coordination, but the
overall flow is implicit across every participant.

</details>

<details id="sync-chain-latency">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--edge">Edge Case</span>Why do calling several services synchronously in sequence hurt latency and reliability?</summary>

Each synchronous call in the chain adds its own latency on top of the others, so total latency
stacks, and the overall call is only as available as the least available service in the chain — one
slow or down dependency anywhere in the sequence slows or breaks the whole request.

</details>

<details id="mixing-sync-and-async">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>Why do most real systems use both synchronous and asynchronous communication rather than picking just one?</summary>

Synchronous calls fit interactions where the caller genuinely needs an answer to proceed (e.g. "is
this payment authorized?"). Asynchronous messaging fits anything that's really a fact being
broadcast that other services should react to on their own time — most systems have both kinds of
interaction.

</details>

## API Gateway & Service Discovery (7 questions)

<details id="what-is-an-api-gateway">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What is an API gateway, and what does it do besides routing requests?</summary>

A single entry point that mediates every external request into a system. Beyond routing, it
commonly handles authentication/authorization, rate limiting, request/response transformation,
response aggregation, and cross-cutting observability, so individual services don't each
reimplement those concerns.

</details>

<details id="api-gateway-vs-load-balancer">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What's the difference between an API gateway and a load balancer?</summary>

A load balancer distributes traffic across instances of the same service using network/HTTP-level
rules. An API gateway operates a layer up — application-level routing across different services,
plus auth, rate limiting, and transformation. A gateway typically sits in front of, and works with,
per-service load balancing.

</details>

<details id="what-is-service-discovery">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What is service discovery, and why can't a service just hardcode another service's address?</summary>

Service discovery is how a caller finds a current, healthy instance of a service by name. Instance
addresses aren't stable — containers/pods are rescheduled, scaled, and given new IPs constantly —
so a caller queries a registry (directly or via an intermediary) instead of hardcoding an address.

</details>

<details id="client-side-vs-server-side-discovery">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What's the difference between client-side and server-side service discovery?</summary>

Client-side: the caller queries the registry directly and picks an instance itself — fewer hops,
but every client needs discovery/load-balancing logic. Server-side: the caller sends requests to a
fixed address (a load balancer or gateway) that consults the registry and forwards — simpler
clients, but an extra, highly-available hop is needed.

</details>

<details id="service-registry-and-health-checks">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What is a service registry, and why do health checks matter for keeping it accurate?</summary>

The database of which instances of which services are currently healthy and where. Instances
register on startup and are removed by deregistering on shutdown or failing periodic health
checks; without active health checking, the registry drifts out of sync and callers get routed to
dead instances.

</details>

<details id="kubernetes-built-in-discovery">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--edge">Edge Case</span>Why might a team running on Kubernetes not need a separate service discovery tool like Eureka or Consul?</summary>

Kubernetes Services already provide server-side discovery largely for free — a stable DNS name per
deployment, internally load-balanced across whichever pod IPs are currently healthy — so many teams
get discovery from the platform without standing up a separate registry.

</details>

<details id="backend-for-frontend">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--edge">Edge Case</span>What is a Backend for Frontend (BFF), and why would a team introduce one alongside an API gateway?</summary>

A dedicated aggregation/transformation layer built for a specific client type (e.g. mobile vs. web),
split out from a general-purpose gateway when different client types need meaningfully different
aggregated response shapes that a single shared gateway can't cleanly serve.

</details>

## Data Management & Distributed Transactions (7 questions)

<details id="database-per-service">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What does "database-per-service" mean, and why is it the default rule in microservices?</summary>

Each service owns its own database, and no other service touches it directly — all access goes
through that service's API. This is what makes services independently deployable: a shared
database would let one service's schema change silently break another, re-coupling them at the
data layer.

</details>

<details id="two-phase-commit">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What is two-phase commit, and why is it rarely used across microservices in practice?</summary>

A coordinator asks every participant to prepare (lock resources) in phase one, and only commits in
phase two if all vote yes. It's rarely used because it's blocking (a slow participant stalls
everyone, holding locks the whole time), requires a shared protocol across all participants, and
trades away availability for consistency.

</details>

<details id="saga-pattern">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What is the Saga pattern, and how does it avoid needing a distributed transaction?</summary>

A sequence of local transactions, one per service, each publishing an event that triggers the next
step. There's no cross-service lock or single atomic commit; if a later step fails, compensating
transactions semantically undo the effects of steps that already succeeded.

</details>

<details id="saga-orchestration-vs-choreography">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What's the difference between an orchestration-based and a choreography-based Saga?</summary>

Orchestration-based: a central saga orchestrator explicitly calls each service and issues
compensating calls on failure — easier to follow, but a central dependency. Choreography-based:
each service publishes and reacts to events with no central coordinator — no single point of
failure, but the overall flow is implicit across participants.

</details>

<details id="compensating-transaction">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What is a compensating transaction, and how is it different from a database rollback?</summary>

A compensating transaction semantically undoes a step that was already truly committed in another
service (e.g. issuing a refund), rather than rolling back an uncommitted local transaction. It's
needed because a Saga's earlier steps are real, independent commits — there's nothing to roll back
across services.

</details>

<details id="outbox-pattern-microservices">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What problem does the transactional outbox pattern solve?</summary>

It solves atomically updating a service's own database and publishing an event about that change —
two separate operations that could otherwise lose or duplicate the event on a crash. The event is
written to an outbox table in the same local transaction as the business change, then reliably
published by a separate process.

</details>

<details id="querying-across-services">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--edge">Edge Case</span>How would you query data that spans two services when you can't JOIN across their databases?</summary>

Either API composition (call each owning service and join in application code — simple but can be
slow) or CQRS with a denormalized read model built ahead of time from events consumed via the
outbox pattern (fast reads, but only eventually consistent with the source data).

</details>

## Resilience Patterns (7 questions)

<details id="cascading-failure">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What is cascading failure, and why does a healthy service become unresponsive when a dependency it calls fails?</summary>

When a called service is slow or down, requests waiting on it pile up and consume the caller's own
threads/connections until the caller itself becomes unresponsive to its own callers, even though it
was healthy. This is how one struggling dependency can take down an entire call graph.

</details>

<details id="why-timeouts-matter">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>Why is a timeout on every downstream call considered a baseline requirement, not an optimization?</summary>

Without a timeout, a hung downstream call ties up the caller's resources indefinitely — often worse
than an outright error, since a fast, clear failure is far easier to handle than an indefinite
hang, and is a direct contributor to cascading failure.

</details>

<details id="retry-storm">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What is a retry storm, and how do exponential backoff and jitter prevent it?</summary>

A retry storm is many callers instantly retrying a struggling dependency, adding load exactly when
it's least able to handle it. Exponential backoff waits progressively longer between attempts, and
jitter randomizes the wait so many callers don't retry in lockstep, spreading out the added load.

</details>

<details id="circuit-breaker-states">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What are the three states of a circuit breaker, and what does each one do?</summary>

Closed: calls go through normally, failures are counted. Open: once failures cross a threshold,
calls fail immediately without attempting the network call, for a cooldown period. Half-open: after
cooldown, a limited number of trial calls decide whether to close again or reopen.

</details>

<details id="retry-vs-circuit-breaker">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--edge">Edge Case</span>What's the difference between what retry solves and what a circuit breaker solves?</summary>

Retry handles brief, isolated blips by re-attempting a call. A circuit breaker handles a dependency
that's sustained-failing, stopping the caller from wasting resources and adding load on a lost
cause until the dependency has a chance to recover — they're complementary, not competing.

</details>

<details id="bulkhead-pattern">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What is the bulkhead pattern, and what failure mode does it specifically prevent?</summary>

Isolating resources (thread/connection pools) per dependency, so one overwhelmed dependency can
only exhaust the resources allocated to calls against it. Without it, a single slow dependency can
starve a shared resource pool and take down calls to completely unrelated, healthy dependencies.

</details>

<details id="retry-idempotency">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--edge">Edge Case</span>Why should retries only be applied to idempotent operations?</summary>

Retrying a non-idempotent operation (like "charge this payment") blindly can repeat its side effect
— e.g. double-charging a customer — because the original call may have actually succeeded and only
the response was lost.

</details>

## Observability (7 questions)

<details id="logs-metrics-traces">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What are the three pillars of observability, and what does each one tell you that the others don't?</summary>

Logs are detailed, discrete events from a single service. Metrics are numeric measurements
aggregated over time, cheap for dashboards/alerting — they tell you something is wrong and roughly
where. Traces follow a single request across every service it touches, narrowing down exactly
where time was spent or it failed.

</details>

<details id="distributed-trace-and-spans">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What is a distributed trace, and what is a span within one?</summary>

A distributed trace represents one end-to-end request as a tree of spans. A span is a single unit
of work with a start time, duration, and metadata; every span shares the same trace ID and (except
the first) carries its parent span's ID, letting tools reconstruct the full call tree.

</details>

<details id="context-propagation">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>How does context propagation let a trace be reconstructed across multiple services?</summary>

Each service includes the trace ID and its own span ID (as the parent) in outgoing request headers;
the receiving service reads those headers, starts a child span, and propagates the same trace ID
onward. If any service drops the headers, the trace breaks into disconnected fragments.

</details>

<details id="correlation-id">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What is a correlation ID, and how is it different from a full distributed trace?</summary>

A single identifier generated at the edge and passed through every downstream call and log line for
a request. It doesn't give span-level timing like a full trace, but lets you grep centralized logs
across every service for one ID — often faster to set up than full tracing infrastructure.

</details>

<details id="centralized-logging">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>Why does centralized logging matter more in microservices than in a monolith?</summary>

Each service instance has its own local logs, and instances are constantly created and destroyed by
the orchestrator — by the time someone wants to debug an issue, the specific instance that logged
it may no longer exist, so logs need to be shipped off and aggregated centrally to be useful.

</details>

<details id="liveness-vs-readiness">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What's the difference between a liveness probe and a readiness probe?</summary>

Liveness asks "is this instance still running correctly, or should it be killed and restarted?" —
failure triggers a restart. Readiness asks "can this instance currently serve traffic?" — failure
just removes it from load-balancing/discovery until it passes again, without restarting it.

</details>

<details id="readiness-failure-mistake">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--edge">Edge Case</span>Why is it a mistake to restart an instance on every readiness check failure?</summary>

Treating a transient, self-recovering readiness failure (like a downstream dependency being briefly
unavailable) as a reason to restart can trigger restart loops instead of simply pulling the
instance out of rotation until the transient condition clears on its own.

</details>

## Deployment & Orchestration (6 questions)

<details id="container-vs-vm">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What's the difference between a container and a virtual machine?</summary>

A container packages an app with its dependencies but shares the host's OS kernel — lightweight,
starts in seconds. A VM virtualizes an entire hardware layer including its own OS kernel — heavier,
starts in minutes, but offers stronger isolation since it doesn't share a kernel.

</details>

<details id="kubernetes-pod">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What is a Kubernetes Pod, and why does it sometimes hold more than one container?</summary>

The smallest deployable unit — one or more tightly-coupled containers that always run together on
the same node, sharing networking/storage. Usually one application container per pod, occasionally
plus a helper sidecar container (e.g. a service mesh proxy).

</details>

<details id="kubernetes-deployment">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What does a Kubernetes Deployment do, and how does its controller loop keep it running?</summary>

It declares how many replicas of a pod should run and manages rolling out new versions. A
controller loop continuously reconciles actual state toward the declared desired state — if a pod
crashes or a node dies, it creates a replacement automatically.

</details>

<details id="deployment-strategies">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What's the difference between rolling, blue-green, and canary deployments?</summary>

Rolling: gradually replace old instances with new, no downtime but both versions briefly coexist.
Blue-green: stand up a full second environment and switch traffic all at once, instant rollback but
needs two environments briefly. Canary: roll out to a small traffic slice first, limiting blast
radius before ramping to 100%.

</details>

<details id="service-mesh-and-sidecar">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What is a service mesh, and what problem does the sidecar proxy pattern solve?</summary>

A service mesh moves cross-cutting network concerns (mTLS, retries, timeouts, circuit breaking,
observability) out of application code into infrastructure. A sidecar proxy deployed alongside
every instance transparently handles that traffic, so application code doesn't need to know the
mesh exists.

</details>

<details id="when-service-mesh-worth-it">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--edge">Edge Case</span>When is a service mesh worth the operational cost, and when isn't it?</summary>

It pays off once the number of services and teams that would otherwise each duplicate
resilience/security/observability logic gets large. Small-to-medium systems can often get similar
behavior more simply with a shared library and a good API gateway, without the extra proxy hop and
operational complexity.

</details>

## Security (6 questions)

<details id="auth-beyond-the-edge">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>Why does authentication in microservices need to be answered at more than just the system's edge?</summary>

A request authenticated at the gateway doesn't automatically prove which internal service-to-service
calls it should be allowed to trigger — a compromised or misconfigured internal service can still
cause damage if nothing downstream re-checks identity and authorization.

</details>

<details id="jwt-fits-microservices">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>Why does JWT's self-contained, locally-verifiable design fit microservices particularly well?</summary>

A JWT's signature can be verified locally by any service, without a network round trip back to an
auth server for every request. An identity provider issues the token once, and every downstream
service can independently trust its claims until expiry.

</details>

<details id="jwt-revocation-tradeoff">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--edge">Edge Case</span>What's the trade-off of a JWT being self-contained when it comes to revoking a compromised token?</summary>

Because it's self-contained, a JWT can't be un-issued before it naturally expires — there's no
server-side session record to delete. Revoking early requires extra machinery like a blocklist the
gateway checks, or deliberately short expiries paired with refresh tokens.

</details>

<details id="zero-trust">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What does zero-trust mean in a microservices context, and how is it different from auth-at-the-edge-only?</summary>

Zero-trust means every service independently verifies the caller's identity and permissions, even
for internal calls, rather than assuming internal traffic is trusted because it originated inside
the network. Auth-at-the-edge-only skips those internal checks, trading defense in depth for
simplicity.

</details>

<details id="mtls">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What is mTLS, and how is it different from ordinary TLS?</summary>

Ordinary TLS has only the server present a certificate the client verifies. Mutual TLS has both
sides present certificates, so the server also cryptographically verifies which service is calling
it — commonly provided automatically by a service mesh's sidecar proxies.

</details>

<details id="api-key-vs-mtls">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--edge">Edge Case</span>Why is an API key generally considered weaker than mTLS for service-to-service authentication?</summary>

An API key is a static shared secret that stays valid until manually rotated and doesn't
cryptographically prove which service is calling — a leaked key is just as usable by an attacker as
by the legitimate service, unlike mTLS's per-connection certificate verification.

</details>

## Anti-Patterns & Trade-offs (6 questions)

<details id="distributed-monolith">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What is a distributed monolith, and how does it end up with the costs of microservices but not the benefits?</summary>

A system deployed as many separate services but still structurally one tightly-coupled application
— services share a database or deploy in lockstep due to tightly coupled APIs. It carries all the
network latency and operational overhead of microservices without independent deployability.

</details>

<details id="chatty-services">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What does it mean for services to be "chatty," and why is it effectively an N+1 problem over the network?</summary>

Completing a single logical operation requires many small, sequential network calls between
services, similar to an N+1 query problem at the database layer. Each call's latency stacks and
each is a new opportunity for partial failure — usually a sign the services belong in the same
bounded context.

</details>

<details id="shared-database-anti-pattern">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>Why is two services sharing a database considered an anti-pattern even if it's convenient short-term?</summary>

It re-couples services at the data layer: one service's schema migration can silently break another
service that was never involved in that deployment, defeating the independent-deployability
database-per-service was meant to provide.

</details>

<details id="strangler-fig-pattern">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--core">Core</span>What is the Strangler Fig pattern, and why is it usually preferred over a big-bang rewrite?</summary>

New functionality is built as new services behind a routing layer while the old monolith keeps
serving everything not yet extracted, gradually shifting traffic over. It avoids a big-bang
rewrite's risk of months of parallel development with no incremental value and a high chance of
never catching up to the still-changing original system.

</details>

<details id="nanoservices">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--edge">Edge Case</span>What are "nanoservices," and why can splitting services too finely be as harmful as not splitting them at all?</summary>

Services split so finely that each is too small to justify its own operational overhead (pipeline,
monitoring, on-call surface). A service should map to a real bounded context, not one class or
table — over-decomposition trades one set of problems for another without a real benefit.

</details>

<details id="when-to-merge-services">
<summary><span className="qb-badge qb-badge--edge">Edge Case</span>How should a team decide whether two services that always change together should actually be merged?</summary>

If two services almost always need to change together and are never scaled or deployed
independently in practice, that's a signal they were split too finely along the wrong boundary and
